HERGUARD • LOVE SHOULD NOT COST YOUR SAFETY

Relationship Abuse &
Control

Abuse can happen in dating relationships, marriages, situationships, former relationships and relationships that never had a label. It is not always physical, and it is not always easy to recognise.

Love does not require fear, obedience, isolation, humiliation or the loss of your freedom. You deserve relationships where respect and safety are not conditional.

♡
Love should make room for your freedom.
CARE WITHOUT CONTROL
UNDERSTANDING ABUSE

What is relationship abuse?

Relationship abuse involves behaviour used to harm, intimidate, control, exploit or dominate another person. It may happen once in a severe incident or develop through a repeated pattern of control and mistreatment.

01

Emotional & psychological abuse

Insults, humiliation, threats, constant criticism, manipulation, intimidation, deliberately creating insecurity or making someone question their own experiences.

02

Physical abuse

Hitting, slapping, choking, pushing, restraining, throwing objects, harming someone or threatening physical violence.

03

Sexual abuse & coercion

Pressuring, forcing or manipulating someone into sexual activity, ignoring consent, exploiting intoxication or making sex a condition of affection, money or staying in a relationship.

04

Financial abuse

Controlling someone's money, preventing them from working or studying, taking their income, creating debt in their name or withholding necessities to maintain control.

05

Digital abuse

Demanding passwords, monitoring location, checking devices without permission, controlling online contacts, threatening to expose private information or impersonating someone.

06

Isolation & coercive control

Gradually separating someone from friends, family, education, work, support systems or independent decision-making through fear, pressure and control.

RECOGNISE THE WARNING SIGNS

Control is not proof of love.

Some unhealthy behaviours are disguised as protection, jealousy, romance, discipline or deep love. The important question is whether you remain free to make choices without fear.

They monitor you constantly

They demand to know where you are, who you are with, what you are wearing or why you did not respond immediately.

They dictate your relationships

They decide who you can speak to, insist you cut off friends or family, or punish you for maintaining other relationships.

They use jealousy as an excuse

They treat jealousy as permission to interrogate, threaten, accuse, control or humiliate you.

They make you afraid of their reaction

You carefully choose your words, hide ordinary activities or change your behaviour because you fear what they might do.

They repeatedly violate your boundaries

They ignore your no, pressure you after you have refused, invade your privacy or treat your discomfort as irrelevant.

They blame you for their behaviour

They say you made them hit you, shout at you, cheat, threaten you or behave cruelly.

They use threats to keep you

They threaten to harm you, themselves, someone you love, your reputation, your finances or your future if you leave or disagree.

They apologise but the pattern continues

They may become affectionate, promise change, buy gifts or express remorse after hurting you, but the behaviour repeatedly returns.

Healthy love does not require surveillance.

Trust is not built by demanding passwords, tracking someone's every movement or removing their privacy. You are allowed to have friendships, personal space, independent thoughts and boundaries in a relationship.

CONSENT & BOUNDARIES

Being in a relationship does not remove your right to say no.

Consent is required in relationships, including marriage. Being someone's partner does not give them automatic access to your body.

You can change your mind

You can agree to something and later decide you no longer want it. Your consent can be withdrawn.

Silence is not consent

Fear, freezing, being asleep, being unconscious or being unable to freely respond is not agreement.

Pressure is not consent

Repeated demands, threats, guilt, manipulation or using money and affection to force agreement undermine consent.

Marriage is not permission

Marriage does not remove bodily autonomy. No one is entitled to force sexual activity because of a relationship, promise or marital status.

You do not owe anyone access to your body.

Not because they bought you something. Not because you have dated for years. Not because you said yes before. Not because you are married. Not because you are afraid of disappointing them.

THE IMPACT OF ABUSE

It can affect more than the relationship.

Abuse can gradually affect a person's confidence, health, finances, education, work, friendships and ability to feel safe.

Emotional health

Anxiety, fear, depression, shame, confusion, emotional exhaustion or difficulty trusting yourself.

Physical health

Injuries, chronic stress, sleep difficulties, reproductive health concerns or other physical consequences.

Independence

Losing access to money, transportation, opportunities, education, work or personal decision-making.

Social connections

Feeling isolated, losing friendships, withdrawing from family or believing nobody will understand.

Self-worth

Feeling undeserving of kindness, believing the abuse is your fault or thinking you cannot do better.

Future relationships

Struggling to trust, fearing intimacy, finding boundaries difficult or carrying unresolved pain.

WHEN YOU ARE CONSIDERING LEAVING

You do not have to leave in one perfect step.

Leaving an abusive relationship can be complicated. Someone may face financial dependence, threats, family pressure, children, housing concerns, fear or emotional attachment. These challenges do not mean the abuse is acceptable.

1

Think about safety first

If you believe the person may hurt you when you leave, avoid announcing your plans without considering your safety. Seek guidance from a trusted person or specialist support service.

2

Identify someone you trust

Consider a person who will support you without informing the abusive person or pressuring you to return.

3

Plan where you can go

If possible, think about a safe place, transport, important contacts, medication, identification, money and other essentials you may need.

4

Consider your digital safety

If your phone or accounts may be monitored, use a trusted device when seeking help. Be careful with changing passwords, disabling location sharing or deleting history if those actions might alert the person or increase danger.

5

Seek specialist support

A domestic violence advocate, counsellor, legal support service or safeguarding organisation may help you consider options and plan for your safety.

6

Remember that leaving is your decision

You deserve help whether you are ready to leave, have already left, are considering returning or are still trying to understand what is happening.

Leaving can sometimes increase danger.

If you are dealing with threats, stalking, violence or coercive control, the period around separation may be especially risky. You are not wrong for taking time to plan. Your safety is more important than someone else's demand that you leave immediately or handle the situation a certain way.

SUPPORTING SOMEONE YOU LOVE

Help without taking away their choices.

When someone you care about is experiencing abuse, your support can matter greatly. The goal is to help them regain safety and control over their own decisions.

Listen without judgement

Let them speak. Avoid asking why they stayed, why they returned or why they did not leave sooner.

Don't force an ultimatum

Avoid insisting that they leave immediately or threatening to withdraw your support if they are not ready.

Respect their confidentiality

Do not share their story, photographs, location or experiences without permission unless immediate safeguarding concerns require action.

Offer practical help

Help them find information, access transport, speak to a professional, identify a safe place or keep important contacts available.

Avoid confronting the abuser

Confrontation may increase risk to the survivor or to you. Consider safety and specialist advice before taking action.

Keep showing up

They may need support more than once. Remind them they are not alone and that help is still available.

GET HELP & FIND SUPPORT

Your safety is important. Your life matters.

If you are in immediate danger, being threatened, injured, trapped or afraid someone may seriously harm you, prioritise reaching a safer place and seeking urgent assistance.

Possible sources of support

  • Trusted person: Someone who will take your concerns seriously and help without exposing you to further danger.
  • Healthcare provider: For injuries, sexual assault, pregnancy concerns, physical symptoms or emotional distress.
  • Legal support: For information about your rights, protective options and available legal assistance.
  • Specialist support organisation: Organisations that work with survivors of domestic violence, sexual abuse and gender-based violence.
  • Emergency assistance: Local emergency services or trusted people who can help you reach safety if you face immediate danger.
