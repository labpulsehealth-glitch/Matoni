Bullying &
Body Shaming

Nobody deserves to be humiliated, threatened, excluded or made to feel smaller because of their body, appearance, background or identity. Bullying is not harmless simply because someone calls it a joke.

You deserve to feel safe in your classroom, home, workplace, community and online. Your dignity is not something another person gets to take away.

UNDERSTANDING THE EXPERIENCE

What does bullying look like?

Bullying is harmful behaviour intended to intimidate, humiliate, control or hurt another person. It can involve repeated behaviour, a power imbalance, or actions that make someone feel unsafe. It is not always loud or obvious.

01

Verbal bullying

Insults, threats, cruel nicknames, mocking someone's voice, accent, intelligence, family, appearance or personal circumstances.

02

Social bullying

Deliberately excluding someone, spreading rumours, encouraging others to avoid them, embarrassing them publicly or damaging their friendships.

03

Physical bullying

Hitting, pushing, tripping, grabbing, damaging belongings, blocking someone's movement or using physical intimidation.

04

Cyberbullying

Online insults, harassment, threatening messages, humiliating posts, fake accounts, coordinated attacks or sharing someone's content to ridicule them.

05

Discriminatory bullying

Targeting someone because of their disability, skin colour, ethnicity, religion, gender, circumstances, perceived differences or other personal characteristics.

06

Bullying through power

Using authority, seniority, popularity, money, influence or dependency to intimidate, silence, punish or repeatedly humiliate someone.

YOUR BODY IS NOT A PUBLIC DISCUSSION

Body shaming is not a compliment, a joke or harmless criticism.

Body shaming happens when someone humiliates, insults or devalues another person's body or appearance. It can happen regardless of someone's size, shape, skin, age or physical characteristics.

Weight & body size

Mocking someone for being thin, fat, gaining weight, losing weight or having a body that does not match another person's expectations.

Skin & appearance

Ridiculing acne, scars, stretch marks, skin colour, facial features, hair, body hair or other natural or visible characteristics.

Menstruation & bodily changes

Humiliating someone for period stains, menstrual products, puberty, breast development, body odour or other normal bodily changes.

Disability & physical differences

Making someone feel inferior, unwanted or less worthy because of a disability, medical condition, physical difference or how they move.

“I was only joking.”

A joke does not automatically become harmless because the person making it laughs. If someone repeatedly humiliates you, ignores your boundaries, or uses your pain as entertainment, you are allowed to take it seriously. You do not have to laugh along to make other people comfortable.

RECOGNISING THE PATTERN

When behaviour becomes harmful

You may begin to notice that the behaviour is affecting how you feel, how you act or how safe you are around a particular person or group.

You are always the target

Someone repeatedly chooses you for ridicule, insults or humiliation, even when other people are treated differently.

Your boundaries are ignored

You have asked them to stop, but they continue, escalate or turn your request into another opportunity to mock you.

People use an audience against you

They wait until others are watching, post about you, encourage people to laugh or try to make you feel isolated.

You feel intimidated or unsafe

You worry about going to school, work, certain places, opening messages or encountering particular people.

You do not have to prove that you are hurt.

You do not need to cry, become angry, fight back or show visible distress for the behaviour to be wrong. Your discomfort is worth taking seriously.

THE EFFECTS CAN BE REAL

What bullying can do to someone

People experience bullying differently. Sometimes the effects are visible. Other times, the person may continue smiling, attending class or going to work while struggling privately.

Emotional wellbeing

Shame, sadness, anxiety, anger, fear, loneliness, loss of confidence or feeling constantly on edge.

Relationship with your body

Feeling uncomfortable in your skin, hiding your body, avoiding mirrors, obsessively comparing yourself or developing unhealthy eating behaviours.

School & work

Difficulty concentrating, reduced performance, avoiding activities, missing school or work, or feeling unable to participate.

Relationships

Struggling to trust people, withdrawing from friends, feeling isolated or believing you have to tolerate disrespect to be accepted.

Physical wellbeing

Stress may contribute to headaches, disturbed sleep, fatigue, stomach discomfort or other physical symptoms.

Sense of self

Beginning to believe the insults, questioning your worth or feeling that you must change yourself to deserve kindness.

IF YOU ARE EXPERIENCING BULLYING

You have options. You do not have to handle it alone.

Your safety matters more than proving a point or confronting someone. There is no single correct response to bullying.

1

Prioritise your safety

If someone is threatening, physically harming or cornering you, Move towards a safer place and people you trust. Do not feel Obligated to confront them.

2

Tell someone you trust

Consider speaking to a trusted friend, parent, guardian, teacher, counsellor, supervisor or another person who can help you take practical steps.

3

Set a boundary if it is safe

You may say, “Don't speak to me that way,” “That comment is not acceptable,” or “Stop making comments about my body.” You are not required to do this if it could put you at risk.

4

Keep a record of what happens

If it is safe, record dates, locations, what happened, witnesses and relevant messages. Keep screenshots of online harassment. Store evidence somewhere the person cannot access.

5

Seek support through an appropriate channel

Depending on where it happens, you may approach school authorities, workplace management, a counsellor, child protection services or another trusted support organisation.

6

Take care of yourself without blaming yourself

Spend time with people who respect you. Give yourself space to recover. Seek mental health support if the experience is affecting your daily life, sleep, confidence or wellbeing.

WHEN IT HAPPENS ONLINE

Protect yourself from cyberbullying

Online abuse can follow you into your home, your private messages and your personal space. You are allowed to protect your digital environment.

Save evidence first, if safe

Screenshots may help document threats, abusive messages, usernames, dates and links before content disappears.

Block or restrict the person

Use available platform tools to reduce contact. Blocking someone is not weakness and you do not owe them access to you.

Report the content

Report harassment, impersonation, threats or harmful content directly to the platform where it occurred.

Protect your privacy

Review who can see your posts, contact you, tag you or access your personal information. Avoid responding if doing so may escalate the situation.

If someone shares intimate images without your consent

This is a serious violation. You are not to blame for someone else's decision to share or threaten to share your private images. Preserve relevant evidence if safe, report the content, seek trusted support and avoid paying or complying with threats. If a minor is involved, do not download, forward or redistribute sexual images of them.

BE THE PERSON WHO MAKES A DIFFERENCE

How to support someone being bullied

Sometimes one supportive person can make a difficult experience feel less isolating. You do not have to solve everything to help.

Listen without judgement

Give them room to explain. Don't dismiss their experience as oversensitivity or tell them they should simply ignore it.

Believe and reassure them

Let them know they deserve respect and that being bullied is not their fault.

Ask what they need

They may need someone to listen, accompany them, help document incidents or support them in approaching someone.

Don't retaliate recklessly

Avoid escalating the situation, exposing private information or organising another attack against the person responsible.

Speak up when safe

You can challenge harmful behaviour, refuse to participate in humiliation or help someone leave an unsafe situation.

Help them find support

If they want help, assist them in reaching a trusted adult, counsellor, appropriate authority or support organisation.

Things you can say to someone who is being bullied

“I'm sorry this is happening to you.”

“You don't deserve to be treated like that.”

“I believe you.”

“Would you like me to stay with you or help you speak to someone?”

GET HELP & FIND SUPPORT

You deserve to be protected, heard and supported.

You do not have to wait until bullying becomes severe before asking for help. If the behaviour is affecting your safety or wellbeing, reaching out is valid.

Where you can turn

  • At school: A trusted teacher, school counsellor, safeguarding lead, principal or responsible school authority.
  • At home: A trusted parent, guardian, relative or adult who will take your experience seriously.
  • At work: A trusted supervisor, HR representative or appropriate workplace reporting channel.
  • Online: The platform's reporting, blocking, privacy and safety tools.
  • In immediate danger: Move towards safety and seek urgent assistance from a trusted person or local emergency service.
  • If your mental health is suffering: Consider speaking with a qualified mental health professional or healthcare provider.
