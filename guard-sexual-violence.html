HERGUARD  /  Sexual Violence & Harassment
HERGUARD · SAFETY · CONSENT

Sexual Violence
& Harassment.

Everyone deserves bodily autonomy, respect, freedom from unwanted sexual attention and the ability to make decisions about their own body. Understanding consent, boundaries and sexual violence can help us recognise harm and support ourselves and others.

A gentle note: This page discusses sexual harassment and violence. Some information may be upsetting. You are free to pause, leave this page or return whenever you feel ready. You do not have to read everything to take care of yourself.
01 · UNDERSTANDING

What do these terms mean?

Sexual violence and harassment can take different forms. They may occur in public, private, educational, professional, social or online spaces. They can be committed by strangers or by people someone knows, including people they trust.

Sexual harassment

Unwanted sexual comments, advances, gestures, requests, messages or conduct that make someone feel intimidated, humiliated, uncomfortable or unsafe. It may happen in person or online.

Sexual assault

Sexual contact or activity involving a person without their consent. Consent cannot be assumed simply because someone is silent, afraid, intoxicated, pressured or unable to make a free decision.

Sexual abuse

Sexual behaviour or contact imposed on someone through force, manipulation, exploitation, threats, unequal power or an inability to freely consent. Abuse can occur repeatedly or as a single incident.

Sexual exploitation

Taking advantage of someone for sexual purposes through manipulation, coercion, vulnerability, unequal power or other exploitative means.

02 · CONSENT

Consent is not a guessing game.

Consent means a freely given agreement to participate in a particular activity. It must not be obtained through fear, threats, pressure, manipulation or force.

No one owes anyone access to their body.

Being in a relationship, being married, having consented before, dressing a certain way, flirting, accepting a gift or being physically close to someone does not automatically mean consent to sexual activity.

Consent is freely given

A person should be able to say yes or no without being threatened, pressured, manipulated or made to fear consequences.

Consent is specific

Agreeing to one activity does not mean agreeing to another. Permission for one thing is not permission for everything.

Consent can change

Anyone can change their mind or withdraw consent at any time. A previous yes does not remove the right to say no later.

Silence is not consent

Free agreement should not be assumed from silence, freezing, fear, lack of resistance or a person's inability to respond.

03 · RECOGNITION

What can sexual harassment look like?

Harassment is not always loud, physical or obvious to other people. It can involve repeated behaviour, a single serious incident, misuse of authority or actions that cross someone's boundaries.

Unwanted sexual comments

Remarks about someone's body, sexual jokes directed at them, persistent comments about their sexual life or unwanted sexual questions.

Unwanted touching

Touching, grabbing, kissing or other physical contact that someone has not agreed to.

Repeated unwanted advances

Continuing to pursue someone sexually after they have said no, shown discomfort or made clear that they are not interested.

Abuse of power

Using authority, grades, employment, money, opportunities, housing or access to resources to demand sexual attention or favours.

Digital sexual harassment

Sending unsolicited sexual content, sexualising someone without permission, making threatening sexual comments or repeatedly contacting someone with unwanted sexual messages.

Image-based abuse

Sharing, threatening to share, or distributing private sexual images or recordings without the person's permission.

04 · POWER & PRESSURE

When saying no doesn't feel safe.

Sometimes people are pressured into sexual situations because another person has power over them, or because they fear losing something important. The absence of physical force does not mean a situation was freely chosen.

Threats

Threatening harm, punishment, exposure, abandonment or other consequences if someone refuses.

Manipulation

Using guilt, emotional pressure, lies or repeated persuasion to make someone agree to something they do not want.

Power imbalance

Exploiting a position as a teacher, employer, caregiver, authority figure or someone controlling access to opportunities.

Fear or incapacitation

Taking advantage of someone who is asleep, unconscious, severely intoxicated, frightened or unable to freely decide.

05 · SEEKING SUPPORT

If this happens to you.

There is no perfect way to react to sexual harassment or violence. People may freeze, comply, run, fight, become confused or feel unable to speak. These reactions do not make what happened their fault.

Prioritise your immediate safety

If possible, move away from the person and towards a safer place. Seek help from someone nearby if you are in immediate danger.

Reach someone you trust

Consider contacting a trusted friend, family member, counsellor, healthcare professional, safeguarding officer or support organisation. Choose someone who will take you seriously and respect your safety.

Consider medical care

If sexual contact or assault occurred, a healthcare professional can discuss injuries, pregnancy prevention, STI prevention and testing, and other care. Some care is time-sensitive, so seeking help as soon as you can may be beneficial.

Preserve evidence if you wish

If you are considering making a report, messages, screenshots, dates, photographs or other records may be helpful. Do not put yourself at risk trying to collect evidence. You can still seek support even if you have no evidence.

Make your own decisions about reporting

Depending on your age, location, immediate safety and applicable safeguarding obligations, reporting options may differ. A qualified local support professional can help you understand your choices.

06 · SUPPORTING SOMEONE

When someone tells you what happened.

Disclosure can be difficult. The way someone is received may influence whether they feel safe seeking further help. You do not need to know every answer to respond with care.

Listen without judgment

Let them speak at their own pace. Avoid interrupting, questioning their decisions or demanding details.

Believe and reassure

You can say: “I'm sorry this happened to you.” “Thank you for telling me.” “I believe you.” “This isn't your fault.”

Don't take control

Ask what they need. Avoid forcing them to report, confront the person, tell others or make decisions before they are ready, while keeping immediate safety and applicable safeguarding duties in mind.

Help them find support

Offer to accompany them to a healthcare provider, counsellor, trusted safeguarding officer or appropriate support service, if they want you to.

07 · LET'S END THE BLAME

Things survivors should never have to hear.

“Why were you there?”

Being somewhere, attending an event or going out does not make a person responsible for someone else's violence.

“Why didn't you fight?”

Fear, shock and freezing are common responses to threatening situations. Resistance is not a requirement for a person's experience to matter.

“But you know him.”

Sexual harm can be committed by someone familiar, a partner, a friend, a relative or someone trusted.

“You should have known better.”

Responsibility belongs to the person who chose to violate another person's boundaries, not the person who experienced it.

YOU DO NOT HAVE TO NAVIGATE THIS ALONE

Support should be safe, respectful and accessible.

If you are looking for help, try to reach a trusted person or a qualified local healthcare, counselling, safeguarding or sexual violence support service. If you are in immediate danger, seek emergency assistance or move to a safer place if you can.

You deserve to be listened to without blame, pressure, humiliation or discrimination.
